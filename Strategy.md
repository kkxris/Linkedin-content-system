## The business

**What Vidushi sells**: Hands-on Claude Code workshops in Berlin for non-technical professionals. In-person, small groups, learn-by-doing format.

**Why it exists**: Non-technical people (marketers, PMs, founders, ops) are being told "just use AI" but have no one who will actually sit with them and show them how. Vidushi is that person.

## Goals (ranked)

1. **Fill each upcoming workshop.** Immediate revenue, immediate signal. Current focus: **July 10, 2025 workshop.**
2. **Build authority as "the person who teaches non-technical Berliners how to actually use AI at work"** — not "AI thought leader," not "prompt engineer." That specific niche.
3. **Personal connection.** People should feel they know Vidushi — her story, her humor, her frustrations — not just her events.

## The customer (ICP)

**Primary audience:**
Non-technical knowledge workers — especially marketers, content professionals, founders, consultants, PMs, operations and creators — who know AI matters but haven't figured out how to use it deeply in their actual work.

**They are:**
- Already experienced at their job; AI is the new thing they are figuring out.
- Curious about AI but often overwhelmed by tools, trends and information.
- Using ChatGPT/Claude casually, but mostly for isolated tasks rather than systems/workflows.
- Interested in saving time, improving their work and becoming more capable with AI.
- Often unsure whether they are "technical enough" to build or automate things.

**Their main problems:**
- Too many AI tools and too little clarity on what actually matters.
- Repetitive, manual and fragmented workflows.
- AI outputs often feel generic or require too much editing.
- They don't know how to move from prompts → context → workflows → systems.
- They feel they may be falling behind but don't want to become developers.
- They consume lots of AI content but struggle to turn it into actual action.

**What they want:**
- Practical AI they can use in their real work.
- Better workflows and systems.
- Less repetitive work.
- Better content and marketing.
- Confidence using more advanced AI.
- Clear explanations without unnecessary technical jargon.
- Real examples from people doing the work.

**What they trust:**
Real experiments, practical examples, proof, specific results, honest lessons and people who actually build/use what they teach.

**What they don't want:**
AI hype, generic tool lists, prompt dumps, jargon, developer-focused explanations, generic productivity advice or empty "future of work" content.

**Core insight:**
They don't need more information about AI. They need clarity on what matters, confidence that they can do it, and practical examples that show them how.

**Geographic layer:**
Berlin is especially important for in-person workshops and Co-Work &, while the upcoming online cohorts will expand the audience beyond location. AI, marketing and content-system content can therefore reach a wider international audience.

**In one line:**
People who are good at their work but haven't yet figured out how to make AI genuinely useful in that work.

## What we are NOT

- Not another "prompt engineering course"
- Not for developers
- Not online-only / not scalable-cohort-based (yet)
- Not thought-leadership-about-AI-in-general

## Positioning

Practical AI for people who are good at their work but haven't yet figured out how to make AI genuinely useful in it.

We are not trying to position Vidushi as a generic AI thought leader or technical expert.

**Example 1 — "post 1 - ai"**

Imagine walking into a restaurant…

and the waiter says: **"Sorry, the table is full."**

Sounds ridiculous.

But that's basically what happens when you hit an AI model's context limit.

Every message, document, image, instruction and piece of conversation you give an AI takes up part of its available context.

Think of it like a table.

The more you put on it, the less room you have left.

And when the table gets too full?

Something has to go.

This is why you might notice an AI suddenly:

Forgets something you told it earlier.

Starts missing instructions.

Loses track of the conversation.

Or gives you a very confident answer that completely ignores something you discussed 20 minutes ago. 🫠 that's why sometimes the AI just needs to clear the table. 👀

**Example 2 — "Post 2 -ai"**

**The internet had a very bad day. And somehow, AI was in the room. 👀**

A critical PHP vulnerability was discovered.

Government websites went offline.

Wikipedia was affected.

Major tech companies were scrambling to check their systems.

And the twist? **AI helped researchers find the vulnerability.**

Which is impressive. And slightly terrifying.

Because AI is making both sides of cybersecurity faster.

**Finding vulnerabilities. And potentially exploiting them.**

That changes the game. 🫠

**Example 3 — "Post 3 -ai"**

**These days, everyone says AI is making us lazy.**

Maybe.

But isn't that kind of the point? 👀

I don't want to spend 40 minutes writing an email AI can help me draft in 2.

I don't want to manually summarise a document.

I don't want to do the boring, repetitive bits just to prove I can.

If AI makes me lazy about **low value work**, I'll take it.

Because I'd rather save my brain for:

**Thinking. Creating. Deciding. Solving actual problems.**

Maybe we're not becoming lazy.

**Maybe we're just getting pickier about what deserves our effort.**

**Example 4 — "Post 4 -ai"**

Four AI models are fighting each other.

Okay, not actually.

But we did put **4 AI models head to head** with the exact same design brief.

Same brief.

Same goal.

Completely different results.

One understood the assignment.

One made something gorgeous but practically useless.

One surprised us.

And one looked like it had never seen a designer in its life. 😂

So we started wondering:

**When it comes to design, which AI is actually the smartest?**

Not the prettiest.

**The one that actually understands why the design works.**

**Example 5 — "Post 5 -ai"**

**not to be dramatic but AI having meetings with other AI is kinda wild. 👀**

Like...

you can give one AI a job.

It does the thing.

Then you can literally have another AI look at its work and go:

**"yeah no. do it again."** 😂

And then another one can check *that* version.

So you could have:

AI doing the research.

AI arguing with the research.

AI turning it into something useful.

And you just...

**show up for the final meeting.**

Honestly, we're getting dangerously close to becoming the person who says:

**"Great work, team. Let's circle back."** 🫠

**Example 6 — "Post 6 - ai"**

**ok hear me out , you're making AI way more complicated than it needs to be. 😭**

Let me make this stupidly simple.

You ask **Gen AI**:

"Write me a launch email."

It writes the email.

You ask **Agentic AI**:

"Plan and execute the launch."

And it can break that into steps.

Research the audience.

Draft the email.

Check the messaging.

Use the tools it has access to.

Review the result.

Then keep going.

So:

**Gen AI creates.**

**Agentic AI acts.**

One gives you the thing.

The other can work towards the **outcome**.

And that's the shift everyone is talking about. 👀

**Example 7 — "Post 7 -ai"**

Your AI strategy might be saving you money.

Or it might just be making your spreadsheet look happier. 👀

Because "hours saved" isn't ROI.

Here's what actual AI ROI can look like:

→ A sales team follows up with 200 more leads and closes 10 extra deals.

→ A marketing team produces 3x more creative and finds a campaign that converts better.

→ A support team handles 1,000 more tickets without hiring another person.

→ A founder gets a weekly analysis in 10 minutes instead of paying someone to spend half a day on it.

**That's the difference.**

AI isn't valuable because it makes work faster.

It's valuable when the time, money or capacity it creates turns into a **real business outcome.**

Otherwise...

You just automated yourself into doing more work. 🫠

**Example 8 — "Post 8- ai"**

Are you one of those people who keeps giving AI the same instructions…

over.

and over.

and over again? 👀

"Write it like this."

"No, less formal."

"Shorter."

"Not THAT short."

"Use this tone."

At some point, you have to wonder:

What if you could just teach the model what good looks like?

That's where fine tuning comes in.

Instead of explaining your preferences every single time, you give a model examples of the outputs you want.

Your writing style.

Your classifications.

Your preferred responses.

Your specific task.

The model learns that pattern and gets better at producing similar outputs.

Think of it like this:

Prompting = telling someone what to do.

Fine tuning = showing them hundreds of examples of how you want it done.

So sometimes the problem isn't that AI doesn't know enough.

It's that you keep teaching it the same thing every morning. 😂

Fine tuning lets you turn that repeated instruction into a learned pattern.

**Example 9 — "Post 9 - ai"**

Take out your pens.

It's back to school time. 👀

Today, let me tell you about RAG.

And no, not the rag we find in dustbins. 😅

RAG is basically an open book exam for AI.

Instead of expecting AI to know everything, you give it access to your own information.

Your docs. Your research. Your knowledge base.

When you ask a question, it looks up the relevant information before answering.

So:

**Fine tuning:** learn how to behave.

**RAG:** here's the information you need.

The AI doesn't need to memorise the textbook.

It just needs to know where to look.

**Example 10 — "Post 10 -ai"**

The most dangerous thing about AI isn't that it can be wrong.

It's that it can be wrong **with confidence.** 👀

Ask it something it doesn't know and it might still give you:

A statistic that never existed.

A source it completely invented.

An answer that sounds so polished you don't even question it.

That's an AI hallucination.

The model isn't necessarily trying to deceive you.

It's generating what it thinks is the most likely answer.

Which means:

**Confident ≠ correct.**

And that's probably one of the most important things to remember when using AI.

**What**: Practical marketing, content and growth lessons from real work.

**Purpose**: Build credibility in marketing while connecting AI to real business outcomes.

**Example 1 — "Post 1 - marketing"**

Someone needs to say this.. so i will.

Posting more won't fix a brand nobody understands.

Your content isn't underperforming. Your positioning is. 👀

I see this constantly.

Teams post daily, weekly, religiously, and still can't explain in one sentence why someone should pick them over the other seventeen tabs open right now.

More content doesn't fix that.

It just repeats the same unclear thing, more often.

If someone can't describe what makes you different after seeing ten posts, the problem was never the eleventh post.

Fix the sentence before you fix the calendar. 👀

**Example 2 — "Post 2 - marketing"**

I think we've collectively forgotten to ask, "Wait, should we even be doing this?" And it's showing.

Briefs get written. Drafts get revised. Decks get approved.

But somewhere in all that activity, nobody asks the most important question:

**Is this actually worth saying?**

We've become so good at producing content that we've stopped questioning the ideas behind it.

Full calendars. Endless output. Very little that makes you stop scrolling.

Sometimes, the problem isn't how fast we're creating.

It's how rarely we're stopping to think. 💛

**Example 3 — "Post 3 - marketing"**

Are you all seeing this, or am I hallucinating? Why does every piece of content sound the same lately?

Same hooks. Same hot takes. Same suspiciously polished paragraphs.

And now AI makes it ridiculously easy to produce more of it.

But if everyone's using AI to say the same things, the problem isn't the writing.

It's that nobody's bringing anything new to the conversation.

AI can help you put your thoughts into words.

It can't replace having thoughts worth sharing.

**The real advantage isn't who creates more content. It's who has something different to say.**

**Example 4 — "Post 4 - marketing"**

You can have 30 posts planned and still have absolutely nothing worth saying. 👀

A content calendar tells you what to post on Tuesday. It won't tell you why anyone should care.

Those are two completely different problems, and most teams only solve the first one.

Filled calendar. Clear schedule. Consistent posting.

Still, nobody's listening.

Because a calendar organises output. It doesn't create a reason to pay attention.

Strategy answers why. A calendar just answers when. 💛

**Example 5 — "Post 5 - marketing"**

More content isn't a growth strategy.

It's just a pile of unwanted texts filling up your storage. 🫠

You can post every day and still be the internet's equivalent of an unread email.

I used to believe the volume myth too. Post more, grow more.

Then I watched accounts post daily and go nowhere, while one sharp, well placed post outperformed a month of filler.

Growth comes from relevance landing somewhere the right people are already paying attention.

Not from how many times you showed up.

Frequency without resonance is just noise with a schedule. 👀

**Example 6 — "Post 6 - marketing"**

Your brand voice is not bold, friendly, and authentic. 😅

Okay it might be. But that sentence won't help a single person actually write like you.

Adjectives describe a vibe. They don't give anyone rules.

A real voice guide tells you what sentence length to use, which words you'd never say, where the humour goes, what gets cut.

Bold is not instructions.

Bold is a compliment pretending to be a system. 👀

**Example 7 — "Post 7 - marketing"**

Imagine automating a mess and then wondering why you have a bigger pile of bad content. 🫠

That's what happens when you add AI to a broken content process.

I learned this building SocialFlo.

If your strategy is unclear, your positioning is fuzzy, and you don't know what you're trying to say, AI won't magically fix it.

It'll just help you produce more of the same, at impressive speed.

**Fix the thinking before you automate the doing.**

AI should make a good process faster, not make a broken one harder to ignore. 👀

**Example 8 — "Post 8 - marketing"**

Going viral and being trusted are not the same achievement. 👀

One gets you a number. The other gets you a customer.

I've seen posts hit huge reach and move nothing. Zero inquiries, zero signups, zero actual business impact.

Attention is loud and temporary.

Trust is quiet and compounding.

Chase the quieter one. It pays better, eventually. 💛

**Example 9 — "Post 9 - marketing"**

Your audience isn't handing out medals for effort. 🫠

You spent three hours on that post. Someone else wrote theirs in 20 minutes.

Guess which one people remember?

Because your audience doesn't see the hours, the edits, or the 17 versions of the same sentence.

They see the post.

And they care about one thing: **does this matter to me?**

Effort gets you to publish.

Relevance gives people a reason to care. 💛

**Example 10 — "Post 10 - marketing"**

Marketing teams don't have a tools problem. They have a seven tabs problem. 😅

Research in one place. Writing in another. Approvals somewhere else. Reporting nowhere, apparently.

Adding another tool to that pile doesn't fix it. It just adds an eighth tab.

What actually helps is fewer handoffs, not more software.

The goal isn't more tools.

It's one less place to lose the thread. 👀

I almost hired a developer before I built a single thing myself.

Felt like the responsible move.

Founder has an idea, founder hires technical expertise, founder gets out of the way.

Then I built the first prototype of SocialFlo myself instead. With Claude Code and way too many tabs open.

**Example 2 — "Post 2 - founder journey"**

Founder life is apparently spending 45 minutes deciding whether a button should be blue or slightly less blue.

People imagine founder life as big vision, bold moves, keynote energy.

Mine looks more like:

Fix a bug.

Test the fix.

Break something else.

Text a user: "Does this feel weird to you?"

Rewrite the same onboarding flow for the fourth time.

Glamorous? No.

Honest? Completely.

There's a very unremarkable middle between "I have an idea" and "I built the thing."

A lot of building is just showing up for that middle.

**Example 3 — "Post 3 - founder journey"**

I used to reply to every message within the hour. (Genuinely proud of it.) 😅

Things I stopped doing:

Answering every DM immediately.

Joining every call "just in case."

Saying yes to every coffee chat.

Checking analytics three times a day.

None of it was wrong exactly. It just stopped being useful once SocialFlo needed actual focus, not availability.

Growing up as a founder apparently means doing less, on purpose. 😅

**Example 4 — "Post 4 - founder journey"**

I made onboarding so simple that users had no idea what to do. 👀

I thought the product should explain itself.

Users opened it. Got confused. Left.

Turns out, "intuitive" is doing a LOT of heavy lifting in software.

So I rebuilt the flow with actual guidance. Short steps. Clear instructions. Nothing fancy.

User satisfaction went up.

The funny part? I'd been trying to make the product look smart.

Users just wanted to know where to click.

A small reminder that building for people means letting go of your own idea of clever. 🫠

**Example 5 — "Post 5 - founder journey"**

The worst thing I could've done was **wait until I felt ready.**

Four months before building SocialFlo, I didn't know how to code.

I didn't wait to learn first. I opened Claude Code and started asking it the things a complete beginner asks.

Broke things. Fixed things. Broke them again.

If I'd waited to feel ready, I'd still be reading documentation instead of having actual users.

Ready is a myth founders wait around for.

Doing is the thing that actually makes you ready. 👀

**Example 6 — "Post 6 - founder journey"**

Getting people to show up is easy.

Getting them to think, "I want to come back" is the real game.

Nobody tells you that the hardest part of building a community isn't getting people in the room. It's getting them to come back.

160+ people joined Co-Work & after six sessions. That part felt easy, almost.

What actually mattered was the quieter signal. People returning without being asked.

Almost 40% of one workshop had already been to a previous one.

That's not a marketing win. That's trust, built slowly, one unremarkable Tuesday at a time.

Community isn't an event. It's a habit people choose to keep. 💛

**Example 7 — "Post 7 - founder journey"**

Had to accept something uncomfortable recently.

Not everyone who loves the idea will pay for the product.

Loving an idea and needing it enough to pay for it are two completely different signals.

I spent weeks collecting compliments and calling it validation.

Compliments don't pay for servers.

The real lesson: ask about money early, even when it feels rude.

Especially when it feels rude. 😅

**Example 8 — "Post 8 - founder journey"**

Before Claude Code, testing an idea meant weeks. A spec, a developer, a queue, a wait.

Now it means an afternoon.

I can build a rough version of something, see if it's actually a good idea, and throw it away by dinner if it isn't.

That changed more than my speed. It changed how many bad ideas I'm willing to test.

Cheap failure means more chances to find the good ones. 🤯

**Example 9 — "Post 9 - founder journey"**

Someone at a workshop once said, quietly, after the session: "I didn't think I was smart enough to build something with AI."

That one sentence changed how I run every workshop since.

I stopped teaching tools first. Started teaching confidence first, tools second.

Because the real blocker was never technical. It was believing you're allowed to try.

That comment did more for Co-Work & than any amount of marketing ever could. 🥹

**Example 10 — "Post 10 - founder journey"**

I built a system to make content creation easier.

Now I'm trying to make sure the whole thing doesn't need me to hold its hand. 👀

Currently trying to figure out how much of SocialFlo should be me, and how much should be the system I built.

Not a lesson. Not solved yet.

Some days the answer feels obvious. Other days I second guess the whole thing mid sentence.

Founders are supposed to have a clean narrative for this stuff.

I don't, right now.

Just a lot of open tabs and an honest "still working on it." 👀

A venue cancelling four days out. A speaker's flight getting delayed. Wifi that decides today is the day to stop working.

Events look effortless because someone absorbed all the effort so you didn't have to. 🫠

The term "Free" gets people through the door. What brings them back is a different story. 👀

I used to think the event itself was the main attraction. The workshop topic, the speaker, the venue.

Then I started noticing the same faces returning.

When I paid attention to what people valued most, it wasn't just what they'd learned. It was the person they'd met, the conversation that stayed with them, the feeling of finding their people in a city as big as Berlin.

The event might bring people together for an afternoon.

The connections are what make it mean something long after. 💛

**Example 6 — "Post 6 - berlin community"**

Everyone was watching AI tutorials. Almost nobody was actually opening the terminal. 😅

Felt like watching cooking videos and calling yourself a chef.

I kept meeting smart, capable people who were terrified of Claude Code before they'd even tried it.

Not because it was hard. Because nobody showed them where to click first.

So I built workshops where people actually build something, in the room, with help the second they get stuck.

Confidence isn't taught. It's built one unstuck moment at a time. 🤯

**Example 7 — "Post 7 - berlin community"**

**A sold out workshop** isn't a marketing win.

It's a room full of people admitting they're scared of something.

Our first Claude Code workshop filled in four days and created a waitlist because of limited space.

That's not demand for a ticket.

That's thirty people quietly saying, I don't want to be left behind by this, and I don't know where to start.

Sold out tells you less about your event and more about what people are actually afraid of missing. 🥹

**Example 8 — "Post 8 - berlin community"**

The best thing that happened at our last workshop wasn't on the agenda. 🥹

It was one participant quietly leaning over to help a stranger who'd gotten stuck, completely unprompted.

I didn't plan that. I couldn't have.

You can design the format, the flow, the timing down to the minute.

You can't design the moment two strangers decide to help each other instead of competing.

That's the part that actually makes people come back. 💛

**Example 9 — "Post 9 - berlin community"**

Nobody has time for one more thing. They have time for the right thing. 👀

Every person I've ever invited to a Co-Work & session had a calendar that said no before I even asked.

So I stopped trying to win the time battle.

I started building sessions people would feel worse about skipping than attending.

Focused work, real connection, something they'd actually finish. Not another optional thing to feel guilty about missing.

People will always make time for something that gives them more than it takes. 🫠

**Example 10 — "Post 10 - berlin community"**

Not everything I've tried with Co-Work & has worked. Some of it flopped, quietly, in front of everyone. 😅

I've planned ideas that didn't make sense. Formats that sounded great on paper and fell flat in the room.

What surprised me most isn't what worked. It's how much repeat attendance ended up mattering more than first impressions.

Almost 40% of people at one workshop had already been to a previous one.

Still figuring most of this out in real time.

Just getting better at noticing which moments actually mattered.

## What deserves to become content

Prioritize ideas that have at least one of these:
- **Useful**: teaches something people can apply.
- **Interesting**: challenges an assumption or creates curiosity.
- **Proven**: comes from a real experiment, result or experience.
- **Relevant**: connects to what the audience is currently figuring out.
- **Distinctive**: has a clear Vidushi POV rather than repeating common AI advice.

Strategy decides WHAT deserves to become content.
Voice decides HOW it gets written.
